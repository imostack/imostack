I came to engineering through design, which is probably why I can't ship an ugly thing in good conscience. These days it runs the other way too — I write the Dockerfile, wire up the pipeline, and own the thing after it goes live. You design differently once you're the one who gets woken up when it breaks.
Six years in, the part I still like most is the small stuff: a form that stops fighting the user, a page that loads before anyone notices it's loading, a flow that just works and nobody writes in to complain about. That silence is the whole job.
<details>
<summary><b>&nbsp;Why I care so much about the boring parts →</b></summary>
<br/>
Because the boring parts are where people quit.
Nobody abandons a product because the animation wasn't smooth enough. They abandon it because the password reset didn't arrive, or the date picker was confusing, or the page went blank on slow data and they didn't know if their money went through.
I've watched real people use things I built. It rearranges your priorities pretty fast.
</details>

App Guts
A small SaaS company where I make practical tools for businesses. No moonshots. Just software that earns its place on someone's screen.
EventsKona — our first product. It handles the unglamorous half of running an event: creating it, selling the tickets, tracking who's actually coming, getting the word out.
<details>
<summary><b>&nbsp;The problem it solves →</b></summary>
<br/>
Most event organizers are running the whole thing out of a group chat and a spreadsheet. Tickets in one place, the guest list in another, payments in a third, and nobody's sure which one is right an hour before doors open.
EventsKona puts those in one place so the organizer can spend their energy on the part people will actually remember.
</details>
